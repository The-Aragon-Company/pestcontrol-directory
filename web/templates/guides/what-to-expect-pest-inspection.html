If you've spotted droppings under the sink, heard scratching in the attic, or just want peace of mind before a bigger problem sets in, booking an inspection is the right first move. Here's exactly how the visit tends to go, step by step, so nothing catches you off guard.

How long does a pest inspection take?

For a typical single-family home, plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Larger homes, homes with crawlspaces and attics, or properties with visible damage can push that closer to 90 minutes. A specialized termite inspection often takes a bit longer than a general pest check because the inspector has to examine wood, soil lines, and moisture more closely.

Apartments and smaller properties usually go faster since there's less square footage and fewer entry points to check. If you're getting a broader home inspection at the same time (say, during a home purchase), expect the pest portion to be scheduled as its own appointment or add-on.

What happens when the inspector arrives?

The visit starts with a conversation, not a checklist. A good inspector will ask what you've seen — bugs, droppings, chewed wood, strange smells — and when you first noticed it. This history helps them figure out where to look first and what pest they're likely dealing with.

From there, they'll walk the property with you if you'd like to tag along, or on their own if you prefer to let them work. Either way, most companies will explain what they're doing as they go, which is a good sign you're working with a legitimate pro rather than someone rushing through to sell a contract.

Which areas of the home get inspected?

A thorough inspection covers both the inside and outside of your home. Typical stops include:

  • Kitchen and pantry — cabinets, under the sink, behind appliances, food storage areas
  • Bathrooms — under sinks, around pipes, signs of moisture
  • Basement or crawlspace — moisture levels, wood-to-soil contact, signs of termites or rodents
  • Attic — insulation damage, droppings, entry points around vents and eaves
  • Garage and storage areas — clutter, stored wood, cardboard boxes pests like to nest in
  • Exterior foundation and siding — cracks, gaps, and weep holes pests use to get inside
  • Yard perimeter — woodpiles, mulch beds, standing water, tree branches touching the roof

Not every visit covers every item on this list — a mouse-focused inspection looks different from a termite inspection or a general pest check. If you're not sure what's included, it's worth confirming before the appointment.

What tools do inspectors use?

Most of what happens during a pest inspection is visual — a flashlight and a trained eye go a long way. Beyond that, inspectors commonly carry:

  • A moisture meter to check for damp wood or hidden leaks that attract pests
  • A screwdriver or probe to tap and test wood for termite or rot damage
  • A flashlight or headlamp for attics, crawlspaces, and dark corners
  • Sometimes a small camera or borescope to look inside wall voids without cutting into drywall

You generally won't see any pesticide applied during the inspection itself — that's a separate step that happens after you approve a treatment plan.

What's in the inspection report?

By the end of the visit, you should receive a written summary of what the inspector found. A solid report includes:

  • The pest(s) identified, or confirmation that none were found
  • Evidence observed (droppings, damage, nests, live activity)
  • "Conducive conditions" — things like excess moisture, gaps, or wood-to-soil contact that invite pests
  • Photos of problem areas when possible
  • A recommended treatment plan with next steps

Keep this report. If you're selling your home, dealing with a landlord dispute, or filing something with insurance, it can serve as documentation of the property's condition at that point in time.

How does the quote process work after the inspection?

Reputable companies give you a written estimate before you agree to anything — usually right there during the same visit. You should see options laid out (a one-time treatment versus an ongoing service plan, for example) along with clear pricing, not just a single "take it or leave it" number.

You're never obligated to sign on the spot. A trustworthy inspector will leave you the quote and give you time to think it over, compare it, or get a second opinion. How should you prepare for the inspection?

A little prep helps the inspector do a more thorough job:

  1. Clear access to the attic hatch, crawlspace entrance, and under-sink cabinets.
  2. Move boxes or clutter away from walls in the garage and basement.
  3. Write down where and when you've seen pest activity.
  4. Secure pets in another room during the walkthrough.
  5. Have any past pest control paperwork on hand if this isn't your first treatment.

Being able to say what you're seeing matters more than most homeowners expect. In our own analysis of 18,941 calls from homeowners to pest control companies between January and July 2026, across 13 pest control companies, we could see how things ended on 7,799 of them. Callers who couldn't name the pest they were dealing with got an appointment scheduled only 8.5% of the time. Callers who named a specific pest — mice, roaches, wasps, termites — ended with an appointment 24% to 31% of the time, depending on the pest. You don't need the exact species. "Small black ants along the kitchen baseboard" or "scratching in the attic after dark" is plenty. A phone photo helps, and if you can safely trap a bug in a sealed jar or bag, the inspector will thank you.

When to call a professional

DIY traps and store-bought sprays can handle a single ant trail or an occasional spider. But it's time to call a licensed pro when you're dealing with an infestation rather than a one-off sighting, any sign of termites or other structural pests, health-risk pests like bed bugs, rodents, or stinging insect nests, or a problem that keeps coming back after you've tried to handle it yourself. A professional inspection also makes sense before buying or selling a home, since undiagnosed pest damage can be expensive to fix later.

Frequently asked questions

Is a pest inspection free?

It depends on the company. Many offer a free general inspection as a way to quote treatment work. Specialized inspections — like a termite letter needed for a home sale — more often carry a fee. If you don't have an urgent problem, it's worth calling around, since a free inspection is a low-risk way to get a professional opinion.

Do I need to be home during the inspection?

Yes, in most cases. The inspector will need access to interior areas like the kitchen, bathrooms, attic, and crawlspace, and it helps to be there to answer questions about what you've seen and point out problem areas.

Will the inspector treat the pest problem the same day?

Sometimes, but not always. Many companies separate inspection and treatment into two visits, especially for larger jobs like termite treatment. For a simple ant or roach issue, some technicians can treat on the same visit once you approve the quote.

What if the inspector finds termites?

You'll get a detailed report on the extent of the damage and a treatment plan, which may involve a specialized termite company if your original inspector doesn't handle termite work directly. Termite treatment often requires follow-up visits to confirm the colony is eliminated.

How often should I schedule a pest inspection?

For most homes, an annual inspection is a reasonable baseline, with more frequent visits in high-risk areas for termites or if you've had recurring problems. Homes in humid climates or older homes with wood-to-soil contact may benefit from twice-yearly checks.

What happens if I decide not to move forward with treatment?

Nothing — a legitimate company will give you the report and quote and let you decide. You're free to get a second opinion, wait, or handle a minor issue yourself. Keep the report either way in case the problem grows.
