What factors affect mice and rat exterminator cost?

Rodent jobs are priced differently than most other pest services because so much of the real fix is physical repair work, not just chemical treatment.

  1. Inspection scope. A full inspection checks the attic, crawl space, foundation, and exterior for entry points, droppings, and nesting signs. More thorough inspections (especially on larger or older homes) take longer and cost more.
  2. Trapping and removal. The number of traps needed and how many visits it takes to clear the infestation both affect price. A light, single-mouse problem is quick; an established colony in the attic takes multiple visits.
  3. Exclusion work. Sealing gaps, vents, chewed corners, and gaps around pipes is often the most expensive part of the job, since it may involve materials like steel mesh, caulk, or even minor construction repair.
  4. Rats vs. mice. Rats are larger, more cautious around traps, and often require more aggressive exclusion work, which can push costs higher than a typical mouse job.
  5. Infestation severity. A few mice caught early costs far less to resolve than an established colony that's been breeding in your walls or attic for months.
  6. Attic or crawl space cleanup. If rodents have contaminated insulation or left significant droppings, cleanup and sanitization can add a separate cost on top of trapping and exclusion.

Published industry ranges put a one-time rodent treatment at $150–$500, with an average around $395. Mice jobs usually land inside that window; rat jobs typically run $150–$600 and can top $1,000 for a severe infestation. A comprehensive job — up to four visits plus sealing multiple entrances — commonly runs $500–$1,500. Those are national ranges, not a quote: your area, home size, and how established the rodents are all change the number, so have a pro price it on your actual house.

How much does each part of rodent control cost?

Inspection: Many companies charge $75–$200 for a standalone inspection, though this fee is often credited toward treatment if you move forward. The inspection identifies entry points and confirms whether you're dealing with mice, rats, or both.

Trapping and removal: Typically $150–$400 for an initial trapping visit, with follow-up visits sometimes included or billed at $75–$150 each until the infestation is cleared. Most light infestations resolve in 2–4 visits.

Exclusion (sealing entry points): This is where cost varies the most — simple gap sealing might run $150–$400, while extensive exclusion work on an older home with multiple entry points can run $800–$1,500 or more. Exclusion is commonly quoted separately from trapping, so ask for both to be itemized. Published industry ranges put sealing a single entry point plus light cleanup at $100–$500, and full exclusion — sealing every gap — at an additional $500–$3,000 depending on how many entry points you have and what materials the job needs. Rodent exclusion doors typically run $200–$400 each. Costs vary by region, home size, and severity.

Attic/crawl space cleanup: If needed, sanitizing and removing contaminated insulation typically adds a separate charge, often $300–$1,000+ depending on the area affected.

What's included at each price level?

Budget-tier ($150–$300): Basic trapping service for a light, confirmed infestation, usually without exclusion work. Good if you've only seen a mouse or two and want them removed quickly, but doesn't prevent future entry.

Mid-tier ($300–$800): Inspection, trapping through multiple visits, and basic exclusion work sealing the most obvious entry points. This is the most common package for an established indoor mouse or rat problem.

Premium-tier ($800–$1,500+): Full-service package including thorough exclusion (sealing all identified entry points), attic or crawl space cleanup, and often a warranty period where the company returns free of charge if rodents get back in.

Is it worth paying a pro for mice or rats?

In most cases, yes — especially for exclusion work. DIY traps can catch individual mice, but they don't stop new ones from getting in through the same gaps, so the problem often returns within weeks. A professional identifies every entry point, not just the obvious ones, and rodents can squeeze through openings as small as a dime (mice) or a quarter (rats).

Rodents also carry health risks through droppings, urine, and the parasites they can bring in, and they can chew through wiring and insulation, leading to costly repairs beyond just the pest problem itself. If you've tried store-bought traps for a few weeks without success, that's usually a sign the infestation is bigger than it looks, or that new rodents keep entering through an unsealed gap. When to call a professional

FAQ

How much does it cost to get rid of mice in a house?

Most homeowners pay $150–$500 for inspection and trapping to clear a mouse infestation. If exclusion work (sealing entry points) is needed, total cost often rises to $300–$1,500 depending on how many gaps need repair.

Is rat extermination more expensive than mice extermination?

Generally, yes. Rats are larger, more trap-shy, and often require more extensive exclusion work, which can push the total cost higher than a comparable mouse job.

Do I need exclusion work, or is trapping enough?

Trapping alone removes the rodents currently inside, but if entry points aren't sealed, new rodents typically get in within weeks or months. Exclusion work is what actually prevents the problem from repeating.

How many visits does it take to get rid of mice?

Most light-to-moderate infestations clear up in 2–4 visits over a few weeks. Larger infestations or those involving rats may take longer, especially if exclusion repairs are also needed.

Can I do rodent exclusion myself to save money?

Simple, obvious gaps can sometimes be sealed with basic materials, but finding every entry point takes a trained eye. Missing even one gap often means the infestation returns, costing you more in the long run.

Does pest control cover damage caused by rodents, like chewed wiring?

No — pest control companies remove the rodents and seal entry points, but they typically don't cover or repair damage like chewed wires or insulation. That's usually a separate contractor. Insurance often won't help either: standard HO-3 homeowners policies typically exclude vermin and rodent damage, usually grouping mice, rats, squirrels, raccoons, and bats together. There's a limited exception — if rodent activity causes a covered secondary peril, like a fire from chewed wiring, the fire damage may be covered even though the chewed wiring isn't. Coverage varies by policy and insurer, so check your own policy.
