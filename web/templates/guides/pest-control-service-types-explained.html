Most homeowners don't know these categories exist until they're already searching for help with bugs, mice, or wasps in their house. Once you understand what each service actually does, it's much easier to know what to ask for when you call around — and to spot when a company is trying to sell you more than you need.

What is one-time pest control treatment?

A one-time treatment is a single visit to deal with a specific, contained problem — a wasp nest under the eaves, an ant trail in the kitchen, a spider infestation in the garage. The technician identifies the pest, treats the area, and doesn't necessarily come back unless the problem returns.

One-time service makes sense when the issue is isolated and unlikely to be part of a bigger pattern. It's usually the cheapest option up front, but it doesn't come with the ongoing monitoring that catches new problems early.

What is recurring pest control service?

Recurring service (sometimes called a maintenance plan) means a pest control company visits your home on a schedule — usually monthly, bi-monthly, or quarterly — to inspect, re-treat problem areas, and apply preventive barriers around your home's exterior. It's less about fixing one thing and more about keeping pests from ever gaining a foothold.

This is the right call if you've had repeat problems, live in an area with heavy seasonal pest pressure, or simply want the peace of mind of not having to think about it. Recurring plans typically cost less per visit than paying for one-time treatments over and over, but they do involve a longer-term commitment — read the fine print before signing (see our guide on pest control contracts).

What is exclusion service, and how is it different?

Exclusion isn't about killing pests — it's about keeping them out in the first place. A technician inspects your home's exterior for gaps, cracks, vents, and other entry points, then seals or screens them so mice, roaches, and other pests can't get in. It's especially common for rodent problems, since mice can squeeze through openings smaller than a dime.

Exclusion is often paired with trapping or treatment, because sealing entry points doesn't remove pests that are already inside. Think of it as the long-term fix that happens after (or alongside) the immediate problem gets handled.

What is trapping, and when do you need it?

Trapping applies mainly to rodents and some wildlife — mice, rats, squirrels, raccoons, and similar animals that pesticide treatments don't work well on. A technician sets and monitors traps (live-capture or otherwise, depending on the animal and local regulations), then removes and disposes of what's caught.

Trapping is a process, not a one-visit fix. It usually takes multiple visits over one to a few weeks to fully clear an active rodent or wildlife problem, especially if exclusion work hasn't been done yet to stop new animals from getting in.

Our own call data shows the line between real, scheduled work and a one-off removal pretty clearly. In a separate set of 12,960 homeowner calls — 5,681 of them about wildlife, and tracked by a different system than the rest of our data, so these figures only make sense compared to each other — the animals that get into a structure were the ones that led to booked appointments. Bat calls ended with an appointment being scheduled 33.6% of the time, skunks 30.9%, squirrels 29.7%, groundhogs 29.5%, and raccoons 28.2%. There's a practical reason for the gap. Bats or raccoons in an attic mean an inspection, trapping, and sealing up the way they got in — that's multi-visit work with something to schedule. A snake in the yard has usually moved on by the time anyone could arrive, and a dead animal is a single pickup with no return visit to book. If your problem is the first kind, expect a real service plan, not a quick stop.

Which service type do you actually need?

Here's a simple way to think about it:

  • A single pest sighting or isolated nest — one-time treatment is usually enough.
  • Recurring bug problems (ants, roaches, spiders) every season — a recurring plan will save you money and hassle over time.
  • Mice, rats, or other animals getting into your home — you likely need trapping paired with exclusion.
  • You've had the same pest come back after treatment — that's usually a sign entry points weren't sealed, and exclusion should be added.

If you're not sure which category your problem falls into, that's normal — most people aren't pest experts, and that's exactly what a phone consultation with a licensed pro is for.

FAQ

Do I need a recurring plan, or is one-time treatment enough?

It depends on whether your problem is a one-off or a pattern. A single wasp nest or an ant trail after a rainstorm is usually a one-time fix. If you're seeing the same pests every season, or in a region known for heavy pest pressure, a recurring plan usually costs less over a year and keeps problems from building up.

Can I combine service types, like exclusion and trapping?

Yes, and for rodent or wildlife problems it's often recommended. Exclusion seals the entry points while trapping removes what's already inside — doing only one usually means the problem returns.

Is exclusion covered under a regular pest control plan?

Sometimes, but not always. Some companies include basic exclusion (sealing a few gaps) as part of a recurring plan, while more extensive sealing work is billed separately. Always ask what's included before you sign anything.

How do I know if I need a specialist instead of a general pest control company?

If you're dealing with termites, bed bugs, or wildlife like raccoons and bats, look specifically for a company that lists that specialty. General pest techs handle common household bugs well, but structural pests and larger animals often require licensed specialists. Our call data backs this up: 19.3% of wildlife calls, 11.2% of wasp and hornet calls, 10.6% of termite calls, and 10.3% of bed bug calls reached a company that doesn't handle that pest — compared with 3.8% for rodents and 3.8% for cockroaches. Ask whether they handle your specific pest before you book.

How long does trapping take to fully solve a rodent problem?

It varies with the size of the infestation, but plan for multiple visits over one to a few weeks rather than a single appointment. Pairing trapping with exclusion work tends to resolve the problem faster and keeps it from recurring.
